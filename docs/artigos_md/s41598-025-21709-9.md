Decentralized adaptive task 
allocation for dynamic multi-agent 
systems
Elizaveta Tarasova1, Victoria Erofeeva1, Oleg Granichin1 & Kirill Chernikov2
We present a decentralized two-layer architecture for dynamic task assignment in multi-agent 
systems, designed to operate under partial observability, noisy feedback, and limited communication. 
The system consists of adaptive controllers that predict task parameters via recursive regression 
with forgetting and selectively broadcast tasks to a small subset of agents based on relevance and 
availability. To ensure consistency of task models across the network, we introduce a distributed 
optimization procedure that combines Simultaneous Perturbation Stochastic Approximation (SPSA) 
with consensus-based synchronization. The proposed approach enables scalable, online task allocation 
without centralized coordination. As a representative application, we evaluate the system on 
simulated workloads involving prompt-based tasks assigned to a diverse set of large language models 
(LLMs), demonstrating its robustness across varying noise levels, task dynamics, and input arrival 
patterns.
Decentralized task allocation in large-scale, heterogeneous multi-agent systems has emerged as a critical 
challenge in domains ranging from robotic swarms and sensor networks to distributed computing and human–
AI collaboration. In such systems, agents independently execute tasks without centralized oversight, enabling 
resilience to failures, scalability to large populations of agents, and adaptability to dynamic environments. 
However, achieving efficient and adaptive task distribution under purely decentralized control is nontrivial: 
agents possess diverse capabilities, tasks exhibit varying urgencies and processing requirements, and timely 
feedback is often delayed or noisy. Conventional approaches–such as auction-based mechanisms, heuristic 
protocols, or market-inspired bidding schemes–typically assume static agent performance models or rely on 
system-wide broadcasts, leading to reduced efficiency and robustness when agent behavior drifts or task types 
evolve.
Dynamic environments pose three principal challenges for decentralized task allocation:
•	 Behavioral drift: Over time, an agent’s processing speed, reliability, or resource availability may change due 
to wear, load fluctuations, or context shifts. Static performance models fail to capture such non-stationarity, 
resulting in mismatches between tasks and agents.
•	 Non-stationary feedback: Task completion outcomes may not reflect historical patterns–e.g., the same agent 
may exhibit different success rates under varying network conditions or environmental contexts–requiring 
continual model updates.
•	 Delayed observability: In realistic settings, agents’ contributions and task outcomes are observed with latency, 
preventing immediate system-wide adaptation and potentially causing local controllers to operate on stale 
information.
Addressing these challenges without resorting to centralized coordination requires lightweight, fully 
decentralized mechanisms for both adaptation and synchronization. In the next section, we review prior works 
on decentralized allocation schemes and adaptive coordination strategies.
Related works
Decentralized task allocation in multi-agent systems has been addressed through a wide range of methods. 
Classical approaches rely on auction-based or rule-driven protocols1–3, which allow agents to make local 
decisions with limited coordination. More recent developments include hierarchical or streaming strategies4,5, 
enabling large-scale deployment and partial visibility. Industrial studies have also emphasized ontology-based 
frameworks for adaptive resource management, where reusable task models support flexibility across domains6.

While effective in static or well-specified environments, such methods often lack robustness under dynamic 
conditions, noisy feedback, or behavioral drift.
Recent advances in consensus control emphasize prescribed-time convergence and event-triggered/self-
triggered mechanisms, ensuring guaranteed agreement within finite horizons while reducing communication 
load. Dynamic and self-triggered protocols have been proposed for nonlinear MAS, achieving prescribed-time 
consensus without Zeno behavior7. Further extensions address leader-following consensus with input saturation 
under a time-varying dynamic event-triggered mechanism, providing explicit lower bounds on inter-event 
intervals8. Related studies have also proposed accumulative-error-based event-triggered schemes for discrete-
time linear systems, where looped functional methods enable co-design of control gains and event parameters 
under input saturation9. These results underline the importance of resource-efficient and resilient coordination, 
complementary to decentralized task-allocation research.
Taken together, these studies illustrate the breadth of existing approaches to decentralized task allocation, 
ranging from control-theoretic consensus protocols to market-based negotiation and machine-learning-driven 
scheduling. To structure the discussion, we next group related work into several methodological categories–
fog computing and distributed scheduling, market-inspired coordination, deep reinforcement learning and 
embedding-based methods, hybrid heuristic and fuzzy logic schemes, metaheuristic optimization, privacy-
aware models, and LLM-based multi-agent coordination. This organization highlights both the diversity of 
solutions and the common limitations, particularly reliance on static assumptions, centralized training, or the 
absence of continuous adaptation to drift and delayed feedback.
Fog computing and distributed scheduling. The fog-computing paradigm epitomizes the very challenges we 
target – resource dynamics, unstable performance parameters, and delayed observability – since micro-data 
centers, routers, and edge devices must self-organize task execution without global coordination10,11. We review 
insights from fog scheduling–including selective task offloading to reduce broadcast overhead, lightweight local 
performance models to accommodate behavioral) drift, and intermittent synchronization under communication 
latency–that are critical for decentralized adaptive task allocation research.
Fully decentralized, multi-agent frameworks have been introduced to handle dynamic workloads 
and unexpected events in fog environments, avoiding single points of failure by local negotiation and 
self?coordination12. In the context of the Internet of Underwater Things (IoUT), a hybrid framework has been 
developed that combines clustering, ant-colony optimization, and a pseudo-trajectory homotopy method 
for unmanned marine vehicles (UMVs)13. The two-layer architecture with surface and underwater vehicles 
illustrates distributed task planning under severe communication constraints, which is conceptually close to 
fog-like environments with intermittent connectivity. In smart manufacturing, hybrid algorithms that combine 
rule?based heuristics with metaheuristics improve makespan and energy efficiency in fog?enabled pipelines14. 
A recent taxonomy classifies these methods by objectives–latency, energy, or load balance–highlighting delay 
and energy minimization as most common11. Machine learning for resource prediction, classification, and 
adaptive control is increasingly adopted in fog nodes15. Federated and distributed learning extend these gains 
to wireless?heterogeneous settings, sharing model updates without central aggregation16. Fair task distribution 
has been modelled as a Distributed Envy Minimization Problem (DEMP), where cooperative algorithms ensure 
Pareto efficiency while minimizing envy among agents with indivisible resources17.
Most fog scheduling schemes either rely on static heuristics–failing to track long?term performance drift–
or incur high communication costs through system?wide broadcasts. For example, dynamic multi?criteria 
schedulers balance urgency, data size, and complexity but lack decentralized feedback loops and predictive 
modelling18.
Decentralized market-inspired coordination. We also examine decentralized market?inspired coordination 
approaches, which apply economic principles to decentralized task allocation. The seminal Contract Net 
Protocol laid out announce–bid negotiation between task issuers and executors, and has been adapted for smart 
homes and multi?robot teams19,20. Recent auction schemes focus on scalability and resilience to communication 
delays. Greedy decentralized algorithms enable real?time bidding with minimal overhead1, while delay?aware 
frameworks incorporate bid quality and timing into winner selection3. Although auctions reduce conflicts and 
support heterogeneity, they often require centralized bid aggregation or global broadcasts, creating bottlenecks 
under high churn or partial observability.
Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) and embedding-based methods. A twin-delayed DDPG model has been 
proposed for offloading delay-sensitive tasks in static fog topologies, where each agent learns task-to-node 
mappings based on fixed observability scopes21. GraphSAGE embeddings have been combined with decentralized 
DRL, but this approach requires a global training phase and prior knowledge of the network graph22. Both 
methods excel in their narrow settings, but struggle to accommodate changing agent sets and partial visibility in 
real time. Task allocation has also been formulated as a potential game, where reinforcement learning with graph 
neural networks enables scalable coordination across large agent populations23. While effective in structured 
environments, this approach depends on centralized training and full task observability, limiting adaptability in 
dynamic and partially observable systems.
Hybrid heuristic and fuzzy logic schemes. Hybrid approaches integrate rule?based heuristics or fuzzy logic 
with metaheuristics or DRL for domain?specific pipelines. For example, fuzzy controllers have been combined 
with genetic algorithms or DRL for smart manufacturing, typically under the assumption of static network 
configurations14,24. Extensions to dynamic fog workloads have also been proposed using fuzzy rules, but these 
approaches lack online learning or feedback-driven adaptation25. These systems optimize makespan or energy 
use but do not generalize well to fully dynamic, non?preemptive environments.
Metaheuristic and optimization-based algorithms. Metaheuristic methods such as the artificial hummingbird 
algorithm focus on minimizing global cost metrics under known workload distributions26. DCOP?based 
schedulers solve constraint networks through precomputed bounds and binary constraints27, while

Our framework directly addresses these limitations through a two-layer decentralized architecture with adaptive 
controllers and SPSA-based consensus synchronization, enabling fully decentralized, online adaptation in 
dynamic environments with noisy and delayed feedback.
Specifically, to manage incoming tasks in a decentralized manner, we propose a two-layer architecture. The 
outer layer consists of adaptive controllers – dedicated decision-making units responsible for analysing tasks, 
predicting behavioral) parameters (e.g., expected processing time, urgency), and selecting a relevant subset of 
agents. The controllers do not directly assign tasks; instead, each task is made visible only to a restricted group 
of agents, who autonomously decide on execution. This limited visibility enables scalability, supports agent 
heterogeneity, and avoids full system broadcast.
Conventional task allocation mechanisms – such as auction-based schemes and heuristic protocols – tend to 
degrade in performance when operating in dynamic environments characterized by changing agent capabilities, 
evolving task types, or inconsistent feedback. These methods typically rely on fixed rules or assumptions about 
system behavior, making them vulnerable to behavioral drift, where agent performance varies over time; non-
stationary feedback, where task outcomes no longer reflect earlier patterns; and delayed observability, where 
task results are not immediately available for adaptation. In contrast, our framework incorporates mechanisms 
for real-time adaptation through localized feedback loops, continuous tracking of behavioral parameters, and 
coordination among controllers. Each controller incrementally updates its predictive models based on the 
outcomes of tasks it observes, enabling robust performance under uncertainty and change.
To balance the distribution of task-handling responsibilities among controllers, we apply a load balancing 
strategy based on a consensus protocol. This mechanism provides a lightweight mechanism that assigns tasks to 
controllers based on real-time estimates of workload and urgency. It operates without centralized coordination 
and is resilient to changing network conditions, making it well-suited for dynamic, large-scale systems.
Once a task is assigned to a controller, local models are used to predict relevant parameters (such as expected 
processing time), and agents are scored based on availability and task fit. Behavioral prediction is performed 
using recursive models with exponential forgetting, which allow each controller to adapt to drift while using 
only its local history.
To maintain coherence between local models across controllers, a decentralized synchronization mechanism 
is introduced. Specifically, we employ decentralized SPSA-based consensus31, which allows each controller to 
refine its prediction parameters via randomized local perturbations and shared function-value observations. 
This synchronization step ensures that controllers do not diverge in their interpretation of similar task types, 
despite working under decentralized and noisy conditions. Importantly, the applied SPSA-based consensus 
method demonstrates consistency under almost arbitrary bounded disturbances, in the sense formalized in32. 
This robustness property is especially critical in real-time online systems, where tasks arrive continuously 
and it is often impossible to repeat experiments or accurately characterize the distribution of uncertainties. 
The importance of developing methods that remain stable and consistent in such non-reproducible, dynamic 
environments has been further emphasized in our prior work33.
Additionally, we evaluate the system in a large-scale simulated setting involving task generation from an LLM, 
highlighting potential applications in knowledge work (e.g., document generation, translation, and analytical 
content creation), content routing, and decentralized language service orchestration.
Building on these design choices, the novelty and main contributions of this work are summarized as follows.
•	 Online adaptation without centralized retraining. Unlike conventional methods that assume static models or 
require periodic offline training, our system explicitly handles behavioral drift, non-stationary feedback, and 
delayed observability by updating task–agent predictions via localized feedback loops.
•	 Two-layer architecture with restricted visibility. Controllers analyse tasks and expose them only to a limited 
subset of agents, which improves scalability, reduces overhead, and avoids system-wide broadcasts.
•	 Decentralized synchronization via SPSA-based consensus. We introduce a lightweight mechanism that aligns 
local predictive models under noise and decentralization, ensuring robustness to uncertainty, an aspect often 
underexplored in existing approaches.
•	 Application to LLM-based workloads. Beyond synthetic scenarios, we validate the approach in multi-LLM task 
execution settings, demonstrating applicability to emerging decentralized knowledge-work environments.
Two-layer decentralized architecture
This section outlines the system structure and task representation, followed by its operational dynamics. The 
proposed two-layer architecture decouples task interpretation from execution, enabling adaptive coordination 
among heterogeneous agents with limited visibility and communication.
System architecture and task representation
We consider a two-layer decentralized architecture designed for real-time task processing in an online setting. 
Tasks arrive over time as external requests submitted by clients, services, or sensors. Formally, we denote the 
task stream as T = {T1, T2, . . . }, an unbounded sequence of tasks arriving at unknown times. Each task Ti is 
characterized by its type, arrival time tarr
i
, and other metadata (e.g., length, deadline, constraints). At each time 
instant, the system has access only to the prefix {T1, . . . , Ti} of tasks that have already arrived by the current 
time; future arrivals are unknown. This online setting imposes specific constraints on prediction and assignment 
mechanisms. The overall structure of the system is illustrated in Fig. 1.
The system comprises two logical layers:
•	 The outer layer consists of adaptive controllers C = {C1, C2, . . . , Cm}, where each controller Cj,

conditions15,16.
Each controller Ci continuously receives feedback from the agents it coordinates. This feedback includes 
actual task durations and agent responses, allowing the controller to refine its internal models. The adaptation 
process is online and incremental: prediction errors are tracked over time, and recent data is prioritized via 
weighted updates. This feedback loop ensures that controllers remain responsive to system dynamics, including 
shifts in agent behaviour, workload patterns, or resource availability.
In addition to prediction and feedback adaptation, market-based paradigms inspire our system. Cognitive 
controllers act as task announcers similar to managers in Contract Net Protocol19, identifying candidate agents 
and distributing tasks based on local information. While explicit bidding is not used, the interaction structure 
reflects the principles of distributed negotiation, where agent visibility, task relevance, and predicted performance 
guide assignment decisions. This hybrid structure allows the system to leverage the robustness of decentralized 
auction-based methods while maintaining lightweight communication overhead1,20.

necessary. Historical metadata used for prediction may be aggregated either over task types or over specific 
clients, if available.

Agents and local state
Execution agents do not have global access to incoming tasks. Instead, each controller selects a subset of agents 
to whom a task is made visible, based on relevance, availability, and internal policies. This visibility mechanism 
is analogous to geolocation-based task assignment in ride-hailing systems.
Each agent Ek makes an autonomous decision about whether to accept a visible task, based solely on local 
information and predefined heuristics. In decentralized multi-agent systems, such local decision-making is 
typically based on utility maximization under partial observability. Common strategies include:
•	 Greedy selection, based on minimizing estimated cost or finish time27.
•	 Earliest Finish Time (EFT), used in dynamic scheduling with local estimates28.
•	 Threshold-based filtering, where agents accept tasks only if predicted utility exceeds a threshold 34.
•	 Auction-style or contract-net mechanisms, enabling agents to bid or negotiate over tasks2,3.
•	 Reputation-aware policies, where agents use historical success or delay statistics to guide selection 35.
These decentralized policies enable agents to coordinate without global synchronization, often achieving 
near-optimal task distribution through emergent behaviour. In our model, agents use a simplified local policy 
combining the earliest availability estimates and relevance scores provided by the controller.
Tasks are executed in a non-preemptive manner: once an agent starts executing a task, it must complete it 
without interruption or reassignment.

Overall system workflow
This subsection describes the sequence of steps involved in processing a task within the proposed decentralized 
two-layer architecture. The operation of the system is organized as follows:

and enters a queue of one of the nearby controllers. The final assignment to a responsible controller Cj ∈C 
is determined via a decentralized local voting protocol, which enables dynamic reallocation across controller 
queues based on current load and predicted readiness. This distributed coordination continues until one 
controller becomes ready to expose the task to a subset of agents. This mechanism ensures:
•	 load balancing among controllers,
•	 robustness to partial communication,
•	 decentralized operation based on local information only.

Step 2: Task Parameter Prediction. The assigned controller Cj analyzes the task using the profile ϕi, along with 
historical execution data and local models. It predicts key behavioral parameters:
•	 expected processing time ˆpi,
•	 acceptable wait time ˆwi,

The prediction process combines global models and local adaptation, allowing both long-term patterns and 
short-term dynamics to be captured.
Step 3: Agent Evaluation and Cluster Formation. The controller evaluates all agents Ej ∈E using two key 
metrics:
•	 Rj,i denotes the relevance score quantifying the compatibility between task j and agent i, typically computed 
from the agent’s profile ηi, where i indexes agents and j indexes tasks;
•	 availability score Aj,i, estimating the likelihood of task completion before the deadline Di.
These values are used to perform clustering in the (Rj,i, Aj,i) space, and a subset of candidate agents is selected 
accordingly.
Step 4: Task Dissemination to Agent Subset. The task is made visible only to the selected group of agents. 
Each agent:
•	 receives the task through the controller,
•	 evaluates it autonomously based on local decision rules,
•	 may use heuristics such as earliest expected completion or minimal current workload.
Step 5: Execution and Feedback. Upon task completion, the selected agent reports back to the controller:
•	 actual processing time,
•	 actual finish time,
•	 task status (e.g., successful, interrupted, or delayed).
This feedback is used to update the controller’s prediction models and refine agent evaluations. Additionally, 
controllers periodically synchronize their internal models using an SPSA-based consensus optimization 
procedure. The detailed mechanisms implemented by the controllers, including task parameter prediction, agent 
cluster selection, and global model correction via stochastic optimization, are described in the next section.
Decentralized adaptive controllers
Task assignment via load balancing protocol
When a new task Ti arrives into the system, it must be assigned to one of the adaptive controllers Cj ∈C. This 
controller becomes responsible for analysing the task, predicting its parameters, and selecting a relevant pool of 
execution agents. Since the system operates in an online decentralized regime without a central dispatcher, task 
allocation must rely solely on local observations and limited communication between neighbouring controllers.
To redistribute incoming tasks among controllers and balance the queues of tasks awaiting assignment, 
we employ a load balancing protocol. This protocol is an iterative and fully distributed mechanism originally 
designed for multi-agent systems operating under dynamic and stochastic communication topologies. The 
protocol enables controllers to reach agreement on load allocation without centralized coordination, while 
remaining robust to partial observability, communication delays, and asynchronous updates.
Applying this strategy requires first defining what constitutes the load of a node (controller), followed by a 
description of the load balancing mechanism.
Controller communication. We assume that controllers are able to exchange control information (e.g., 
workload estimates, task-urgency metrics) and collaboratively rebalance task assignments among neighboring 
controllers. In fog-computing environments, permanent all-to-all connections are typically infeasible: links may 
be unavailable at certain times due to bandwidth limitations, interference, or traffic constraints. Even when 
multiple neighbors are accessible, maintaining simultaneous communication with all of them is often impractical.

Experimental validation through LLM-based scenarios
To assess the effectiveness of our approach in LLM-related settings, we simulate a decentralized two-layer 
system operating on batches of prompts processed by large language models (LLMs). The architecture is not 
tailored to LLMs per se but supports their heterogeneity and dynamic behavior. The goal of this evaluation is 
to demonstrate how adaptive controllers can learn task-agent mappings, coordinate assignments, and maintain 
accurate performance predictions across diverse prompt types and execution conditions.
The simulation environment involves three key components:
•	 Tasks, grouped into batches of prompts with varied structure, semantic categories, and complexity levels. 
Each task is described by a feature vector used in local prediction models.
•	 Agent-executors, instantiated as heterogeneous LLMs with different capabilities and success rates across 
prompt types. These agents serve as the lower layer of the system.
•	 Adaptive controllers, forming the upper coordination layer, responsible for predicting task parameters, se­
lecting candidate agents, and coordinating assignment using local information and decentralized consensus.
All modelled configurations employ an adaptation algorithm based on Simultaneous Perturbation Stochastic 
Approximation (SPSA) with a consensus mechanism presented above. Gradient estimation follows a two-point 
scheme applied at each parameter update step. The experiments use the following hyperparameters: step-size 
α = 0.01, perturbation scale β = 0.1, consensus step-size γ = 0.02, and parameter updates are performed 
every 10 tasks. These values provide a balance between learning stability and responsiveness to local variations 
in the environment.
The following sections describe two independent simulation scenarios, differing in the realism of agent 
and task representations, as well as in how relevance and execution time are defined. The first scenario models 
a synthetic environment with abstract agents and simulated execution times. The second involves real-time 
interaction with LLM APIs and measurement of actual task completion times; this scenario also includes a 
comparison between the proposed method and a round-robin baseline.
Synthetic simulation
To evaluate the core properties of our task allocation system in a controlled setting, we conducted a synthetic 
simulation. The environment allowed us to explicitly define task characteristics, agent capabilities, and 
observation noise, enabling a transparent assessment of system behaviour under varied conditions.
Task and agent setup. Each task was represented as a prompt of a certain type, sampled from one of six 
categories: Data Analysis, Programming, Translation, Summarization, Q&A, and Creative Writing. Tasks arrived 
dynamically in batches of size 1 to 8, and each batch could contain a heterogeneous mix of task types. Each 
task was associated with a release time ri, complexity score h(Ti), and type typei. The feature vector ϕi was 
generated as a real-valued vector of fixed dimensionality.
The system simulated five synthetic agents representing major LLM classes: GPT-4, Claude-3.5, 
Gemini-1.5, LLaMA-3, and Mistral-7B. These agents acted as behavioural proxies with adaptive 
performance characteristics. Rather than using static efficiency profiles, each agent’s success probability and 
response latency were dynamically updated during the simulation based on observed task outcomes.
Controller logic. Four adaptive controllers were used, connected in a sparse communication graph. For each 
task, a controller predicted the expected execution time ˆpi and acceptable waiting window ˆwi. The deadline was 
then computed as Di = ri + ˆwi + ˆpi.
Based on availability and current relevance, a candidate pool of agents was formed. The controller did not 
assign a specific agent; instead, agents in the pool independently claimed tasks, mimicking multi-agent selection 
dynamics. Controllers also coordinated through a Local Voting Protocol (LVP) for distributing tasks among 
themselves. Model parameters were updated via SPSA with consensus.

uncertainty environments.
The proposed framework integrates predictive models for task parameters, relevance- and availability-based 
agent selection, and decentralized coordination among controllers. We further enhance adaptability through

Simulation results demonstrate the effectiveness of the architecture on real-world prompt-like tasks 
distributed among diverse large language models (LLMs). The system outperforms baseline approaches in both 
average performance and robustness across a variety of task categories. The second set of experiments confirms 
that the proposed adaptation mechanism enables stable online learning in the presence of structural drift and 
noise.
Beyond its empirical performance, the novelty of this work lies in explicitly addressing three persistent 
gaps in decentralized task allocation: (i) enabling online adaptation under behavioral drift and noisy feedback 
without centralized retraining, (ii) introducing a two-layer architecture with selective task visibility to support 
scalability and heterogeneity, and (iii) providing decentralized synchronization via SPSA-based consensus 
under bounded disturbances. Furthermore, by validating the framework on LLM-driven workloads, we 
demonstrate its applicability to emerging domains of decentralized knowledge work, where adaptability and 
robustness are critical. These aspects distinguish our approach from conventional auction-based, heuristic, or 
DRL-heavy methods and establish a foundation for scalable and resilient task allocation in dynamic multi-agent 
environments.
Future work will focus on integrating hierarchical control and reward-based feedback, enabling agent 
selection not only based on availability and predicted accuracy but also on long-term outcomes. Additionally, 
we plan to explore task allocation in resource-constrained or partially observable settings, further pushing 
the boundary of adaptive multi-agent coordination. While the current architecture focuses on decentralized 
adaptation and agent selection, it can be further extended toward orchestration-level control. By introducing 
additional coordination layers or hierarchical planning mechanisms, the system could support more complex 
task dependencies, agent negotiations, and global workflow management – enabling orchestration without 
compromising its decentralized foundation.
